## The principles

Four, drawn from both authentic relating and circling. Spoken between the two
of you rather than read out, with one real example each. Not a final exam, an
offering.

**1. Welcome everything, trust your experience.**
Not trying to change your experience into something else. Opening your
perception, observing what is, without judgment, with curiosity. Giving it
space rather than shoving parts away because they feel wrong to have. Some
parts we were conditioned not to welcome, anger for instance, and that is often
exactly where the growth is. Alongside welcoming there is trusting: it can be
hard to trust shame or discomfort and let it have room. *Okay, I am feeling it
right now, that is what is present.* Whenever it gets abstract, the body brings
you back.

**2. Present moment focus, and not going into stories.**
The first default for most of us is the story and the explanation. Here we
offer dropping into the present instead, and the easiest door is the body.
Three things worth telling apart:

- **Sensation.** My stomach is growling. My cheeks are hot.
- **Feeling.** I am angry. I am upset. I am warm toward you.
- **Story.** What I make it mean, about myself or about you.

Stories are not forbidden, they are simply what carries us out of the present
moment and out of contact with the feeling underneath.

**3. Own your experience, reveal it, share the impact.**
The feeling is mine, I do not put it on anyone else. Noticing it inside is one
thing; speaking it is the step toward vulnerability, and that step is what we
welcome most. Sharing the impact is the other half: *I feel X when you Y*,
never a verdict on the other person. And if the urge to rescue someone arrives,
that is the material: *I notice a strong wish to help you right now.*

**4. Do not fix, change, or rescue.**
Honour each person's sovereignty and their capacity to hold their own state.
Resist advice, cheering up, minimising discomfort, solving an emotion. The urge
to fix is real and worth revealing. It is the acting on it that closes the
space.

Said aloud at the welcome, alongside the four: **you always choose how much to
share**, passing is a full answer, you can step out and come back, and what is
said here stays here.

## The beats in detail

### Welcome and frame (8 min)

Who you both are, a sentence each. Why you run this, something true rather than
a pitch. The shape of the evening in four beats so nobody is guessing. Then the
permissions and the practicalities: toilets, break time, phones away.

Naming your own nervousness here, if it is there, does more for the safety of
the room than any framing does.

### Opening circle (20 min)

Everyone speaks once, sitting in the circle. Quite a bit of structure, so
nobody has to invent what to say:

- Your name.
- **Three sensations** you can feel in your body right now.
- **What you are arriving with,** what it is like to be you at this moment.
- **What brought you,** your curiosity or desire for today. Your intention.

Demonstrate it by going first, at the length you want back. Around a minute
each.

**Scaling.** Twelve people at a minute breathes. Seven is seven minutes, and
the minutes you save are worth giving to the closing circle, which needs them.
Only above about sixteen does this round need cutting, by dropping to two
sensations or splitting into two half circles with one of you in each.

### The principles (18 min)

Two each, so it sounds like a conversation between you rather than a briefing.
Roughly four minutes per principle with one real example from your own life.

Land sensation, feeling, story at the end. That is the distinction people
actually use in the pair exercises, so write it on a sheet of paper and leave
it visible.

This is the longest talking block of the evening. If the room goes flat, cut
the fourth principle short and let the exercises teach it instead.

### Breathing (6 min)

Arriving on the ground first. Slow, full breaths, filling all the way up, a
little more than feels finished, sometimes two breaths in. Then the long, slow
release. Around eight cycles, unhurried.

### Grounding meditation (10 min)

Feeling the ground, then travelling through the body and letting each part
relax: fingernails, hands, feet, ankles, pelvis, chest, head. Not a scan you
rush. At each place the question is gentle and slow, what is it like to feel
this part relaxing, then staying with that a little.

**The script.** Spoken slowly, with pauses longer than feel natural. The words
are a shape to speak from, not a text to recite. Built on Dmitrijs's own
spoken draft, merged with the MBSR lineage of body scans; the sources and what
was borrowed are in `research/body-scan-transcripts/notes.md`.

> Let yourself settle. I invite you to lie back on the ground, or stay
> seated, whatever your body wants right now. If closing the eyes feels good,
> let them close. And if at any point you would rather have them open, that is
> welcome too. The room stays here, and you can come back to it whenever you
> like.
>
> The invitation tonight is to fall into your presence. Feel the places where
> your body touches the ground. The ground is already holding you. There is
> nothing you have to do to earn that. And let the breath be as it is. No need
> to do anything about it. Feel what it is like to be in your body, right
> here.
>
> For the next few minutes there is nowhere to get to and nothing to achieve.
> We are going to travel through the body, slowly, and simply visit each part.
> Whatever you find there is welcome: warmth, coolness, tingling, pressure,
> heaviness, or nothing much at all. Nothing much is also an answer. There is
> no right way to feel.
>
> Begin all the way down at the toes. The little toes. What happens when we
> put our attention there? Is it quiet, or is there some life going on, some
> aliveness? Either one is a full answer.
>
> Take a slightly deeper breath, and as it leaves, let the attention spread
> through the whole of the feet, the soles, the heels, up into the ankles.
> What is it like to let the feet relax? Stay with that a moment.
>
> Then the shins, and behind them the calves, and the knees. The legs have
> carried you all day, and now they get to do nothing at all. If somewhere
> feels tight, you do not have to change it. Rest your attention there, warm
> and unhurried, and gently invite relaxation.
>
> Another easy breath, and as it goes, come up through the thighs, into the
> pelvis and the glutes. Let the whole weight of it sink toward the ground.
> This is the base of the body, and it can be heavy here, fully supported.
>
> The stomach. Let it be soft, and let the breath move it however it wants.
> Here I invite some curiosity: if you meet something new or unexpected, a
> feeling, something without a name, greet it with warmth. It can stay. Find
> peace with the way it is right now.
>
> And if by now the mind has wandered off, to the day, to a story, to
> somewhere else entirely, nothing has gone wrong. That is what minds do.
> Notice where it went, and walk it back gently. As many times as it takes.
> Each return is the practice.
>
> Now travel up the midline. The lower back, the spine, and the space between
> the shoulder blades. If it feels good to move a little here, move a little.
> You can imagine water, or a soft touch, resting on the shoulder blades and
> carrying away some of their weight. Feel the weight, if there is any, and
> let what wants to go, go.
>
> The chest, and the heart. Feel the expansion as the air comes in. Notice
> what the weather is like in there at this moment. Whatever you meet, it does
> not need to be different. See if you can stay beside it the way you would
> sit beside a friend: not fixing anything, just present, just company.
>
> One more slow breath, and as it leaves, let the attention pour down the
> arms, the feeling of the skin, through the elbows and the wrists, into the
> hands. The palms, each finger, all the way out to the fingernails. What is
> it like to feel the hands completely at rest?
>
> The neck. Notice how it is, and if it wants to move, let it move. The jaw:
> let it unclench, let the teeth part slightly. The cheekbones, the nose, the
> small muscles behind the eyes. The forehead. The whole head, resting.
>
> Now feel the body as one whole, from the toes to the top of the head, here,
> breathing by itself. Sensations and feelings arriving, unfolding, passing
> by, and you here with them. If a quiet thank you toward this body wants to
> happen, let it. Nothing more is asked of you.
>
> *(a long pause here, up to a minute if the room is settled)*
>
> When you are ready, let the breath grow a little deeper. Small movements in
> the fingers and the toes. Take your time, and in your own time, let the eyes
> open, without landing on anything in particular. Let the room arrive slowly.

If someone looks flooded, the invitation is always eyes open, finding the room
again, and that interrupting the exercise for yourself is completely fine.
Taking care of yourself is the practice working, not the practice failing.

This beat is the handover from talking to moving. Keep it soft rather than
deep. The depth belongs later.

### Rising, ground and water (10 min)

Coming up onto the feet slowly. Moving with the ground and with the water:
rotations, following different parts, the feet, the hands, the head. A little
stretching. The quality is grounded and watery, the flowing part of the
evening. Solo movement, no contact yet. Music does most of the work.

### Shaking (8 min)

Hands, arms, legs, the whole body. Loose and relaxed rather than effortful.
Five minutes minimum, eight if the room is with it.

The real gift is that everyone gets to look ridiculous together in the first
hour. That permission is worth more than the shaking.

### Walking and meeting (10 min)

Walking with yourself first. Then walking in the space, noticing there are
other people in it. Then the invitation to greet someone physically and
lightly: a greeting with the feet, with the elbows, whatever wants to happen.
Feel what it is like to meet with an elbow. Light and easy, several meetings,
nothing held for long.

It closes standing back to back with one person, resting there, breathing.

**Frame consent before this starts.** The invitation, how to decline, and the
fact that walking past someone is a complete and welcome answer. Thirty seconds
of framing changes how the whole segment feels for anyone who came alone.

### Settling into sitting (5 min)

From back to back, sliding down to sitting with the same person. Stay back to
back a moment, then turn to face each other. This is the first pair, no partner
picking required.

### Pairs one: staying with my experience (15 min)

The three layers, out loud.

- **3 min.** A speaks, B only listens. Present tense: *a sensation I notice…
  a feeling I notice… a thought I notice…* Silence between is welcome.
- **3 min.** Swap.
- **7 min. Ping pong.** One line each, alternating. *Right now I notice…* No
  reply required, no conversation.

Demonstrate it, the two of you, for thirty seconds. Explaining takes three
minutes and lands worse. With an odd number, one of you partners up and the
other keeps time.

### Break (15 min)

Not dead time. This is where people who came alone stop being alone. Tea,
water, fruit, and both of you circulate toward whoever is standing on their
own. A fifteen minute break becomes twenty five without somebody calling people
back.

### Pairs two: sharing the impact (15 min)

New partner. Same ping pong with one thing added: what happens in me when you
say yours.

- A: *Right now I notice…*
- B: *Hearing that, I notice…* then their own.
- Alternating, about 12 minutes, switching who opens halfway.

Name two things before it starts. The impact is always from my side, *I feel X
when you Y*, never a read on who they are. And if the urge to help or fix
arrives, say it instead of doing it.

If you want the curiosity principle in the evening, this is where it fits: add
*you seem [ ] to me, is that true for you?* as a third permitted move.

### Threes (25 min)

Groups of three, counted off rather than self selected. One person is the
focus, the other two are with them.

- The focus person says what is alive for them right now. Not a story from the
  week, what is here.
- The other two say what they notice **in themselves** while being with that
  person, and may ask what is here for them now.
- No advice, no fixing, no going into the past.
- Around seven minutes each, then a short pause and rotate.

This is the bridge into circling. Say that, so people know the last beat is not
a new thing to learn. Both of you float rather than sitting in one group, and
ring a bell at each rotation so the groups stay together in time.

Seven people makes one three and one four, and the four gets about five minutes
each rather than seven. **If only seven or eight arrive,** staying in one circle
for this beat is the better evening, and it turns the last two beats into one
long circle.

### Full circle and close (15 min)

Everyone back into one circle. Open and unscripted: whoever is moved to speak,
speaks. Same principles, no turns, no structure. One of you holds it lightly.

At around 19:24, bring it to the close. One sentence each, somatic: how am I
right now, what am I taking with me. Model it short by going first. Then thank
people, mention the next evening if there is one, and let the room stay for
tea.

**This is the tightest moment in the plan.** At seven to twelve people the
closing round costs four to six minutes, which leaves about ten for the open
circle. Enough for a taste of it, not for a real one. If the free flowing circle
is the part you most want, take five minutes from the threes and five from the
principles and give this beat twenty five.

## Still to settle

1. **The pair exercises in detail.** What is written above is one workable
   version. Open: whether ping pong is the right form, whether both rounds
   change partners, and whether an eye contact or silence beat belongs between
   them.
2. **Touch in the walking segment.** Elbows, feet and back to back are in the
   plan. In a room of strangers this needs an explicit consent frame and an opt
   out that costs nothing. Who frames it, in what words, and does back to back
   stay if the room feels shy?
3. **Four principles or five.** Curiosity instead of conclusion is the
   strongest missing candidate: the present moment principle handles the story
   I tell about myself, not the story I tell about you. Either a fifth
   principle at the open, which costs four more minutes of talking, or a move
   inside pairs two, which costs nothing.
4. **How many people.** Aiming for seven to twelve, and the room holds about
   twelve, but the number will not be known until the day. Rather than guessing,
   agree the two branches: what changes if seven arrive, and what changes if
   sixteen do. The scaling notes on the opening circle and on the threes are the
   two places it bites.
5. **Music.** Arrival, ground and water, shaking, and something quiet for the
   break. Diana curates. The app's player still defaults to the Portugal track,
   Nicola Cruz, "Cumbia del Olvido".
6. **Who owns the clock.** Pick one person now.
7. **Tea, fruit, cups, cushions, mats,** and who brings them.
8. **What you want from the evening for yourselves.** Worth five minutes. You
   will facilitate differently if you know.
